No.48

After Seeing the Darkest Side of Human Nature, Should We Still Trust People?

Compassion can be boundless, but trust must have measure — keeping wisdom and boundaries for kindness.

Author's record · translated; the Chinese original is authoritative

Open in reading mode →

🔶Inquiry to Ksitigarbha Bodhisattva

I asked Ksitigarbha Bodhisattva: after seeing so much selfishness, betrayal, violence and deception, should one still believe in human nature? How can one be neither naive, nor lose compassion and faith in beings because of it?

🔶The Bodhisattva's Teaching

You see the evil in the human world and ask whether you can still trust people.

This question does not arise because you lack compassion, but because the suffering you have seen grows deeper.

Beings have good and also evil; they have compassion and also greed, anger and delusion. So the practitioner need not believe that everyone is good in order to be compassionate. To know that someone may do evil, and still not wish to treat them with evil — this is compassion. If you clearly know they deceive and still let them deceive, this is not compassion; it is lack of wisdom.

If you clearly know they harm and still do not avoid the harm, this is not kindness; it is a failure to protect.

The Dharma does not teach you to take evil people for good people; nor does it teach you, because you see evil people, to decide that all beings are evil. What you should believe is not that 'everyone can be trusted'; what you should believe is that the hearts of beings are not forever unchanging. The greedy today may come to know contentment; the angry today may come to repent; the deluded today may also awaken. Yet while they have not yet changed, you must still guard against their evil.

Compassion can be boundless; trust must have measure. Compassion is wishing them free of suffering; trust must be proven by their conduct. So you may be compassionate toward someone, and still not lend them money; you may wish someone to turn toward good, and no longer work with them; you may forgive someone, and not let them hurt you again; you may hold no hatred, and still keep your distance. None of this goes against compassion. If, because you were once deceived, you never trust anyone again, then one person's evil makes countless people who never harmed you suffer your suspicion first.

If, wanting to believe in human nature, you ignore every evil deed,
then compassion becomes delusion.
So learn:
see good without blind belief, see evil without despair.

In judging a person, observe their long-term conduct; when you hear their words, see whether they are put into practice.

Give trust gradually; set boundaries first. A single deception may be forgiven; whether to trust again depends on whether they have truly changed. That beings are worthy of compassion does not mean all beings are worthy of trust. What you must truly guard is not a fantasy that 'human nature must be good'; it is that, having seen all the darkness of the world, you do not let the darkness enter your own heart. Seeing others selfish, you still learn to give; seeing others betray, you still keep faith; seeing others violent, you do not meet violence with violence; seeing others deceive, you still keep to the truth.

In this way, the evil of the world has not turned you into the kind of person you detest. This is rare indeed. Not naive — that is wisdom; not despairing — that is compassion. Know evil and guard against evil, see suffering and pity suffering; do not indulge, nor hate. To walk like this is the Middle Way.

🔶Plain Rendering

You see all kinds of evil in the human world, and then ask: "Should I still believe in human nature?" This question arises not because you lack compassion, but because you have seen more and more suffering. Among beings there is goodness, and there is also evil; there is compassion, and there is also greed, anger and delusion. So practitioners do not need to treat every person as good in order to be compassionate toward people. Knowing someone might do wrong, yet still not wanting to repay wrong with wrong — that is what compassion is.

If you know someone is deceiving you and let them go on deceiving you, that is not compassion; that is lacking wisdom.

If you know someone will bring harm and do not protect yourself, that is not kindness; that is a lack of protection.

The Dharma does not teach you to "treat bad people as good people"; nor does it teach you to "decide all beings are evil because you have seen bad people". What is truly worth believing is not that "everyone is trustworthy"; what is truly worth believing is that "people's hearts are not forever unchanging". Those who are greedy today may later learn contentment; those who are angry today may later repent; those who are confused today may also later awaken. But until they have changed, you still need to guard against their wrongdoing.

Compassion can be unlimited, but trust must have limits. Compassion is "hoping they are free of suffering"; trust must be proven by their actions. So you can be compassionate toward someone and still not lend them money; you can hope someone turns toward good and still no longer work with them; you can forgive someone and still not let them hurt you again; you can hold no resentment and still keep your distance. None of these go against compassion. If, because you were once deceived, you stop trusting anyone at all, then one person's wrong ends up making countless people who never hurt you bear your suspicion first.

If, for the sake of "believing in human nature", you ignore every warning sign of wrongdoing, then your compassion turns into foolishness. So learn to see goodness without believing blindly, and see evil without falling into despair. To judge a person, look at their behavior over a long time; when you hear their words, see whether they actually follow through. Give trust slowly, and set boundaries first.

One deception can be forgiven; but whether to trust again depends on whether they have truly changed. That beings deserve your compassion does not mean every being deserves your trust. What you should truly hold on to is not the fantasy that "human nature must be good"; it is not letting the darkness move into your own heart because you have seen so much darkness in the world. When you see others being selfish, keep learning to give; when you see others betray, keep your word; when you see others being violent, do not answer violence with violence; when you see others deceive, keep holding to the truth.

In this way, the evil of the world has not turned you into the kind of person you dislike. That is what is rare. Not being naive comes from wisdom; not despairing comes from compassion. Know evil and guard against it, see suffering and feel for it; do not indulge it, and do not hate. To live like this is the Middle Way.
