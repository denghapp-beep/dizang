No.51

Compassion Need Not Mean Closeness; Filial Piety Can Have Boundaries

When family relationships cause harm, asking about filial piety, distance and protecting oneself.

Author's record · translated; the Chinese original is authoritative

Open in reading mode →

🔶Inquiry to Ksitigarbha Bodhisattva

I asked Ksitigarbha Bodhisattva:

"We often hear of children who do not get along with their parents — children mocked, scolded and verbally controlled by their parents for years, or who later become unfilial because of it.

From the Buddhist point of view, is this the past-life karma of parent and child (collecting and repaying debts)?

When facing parents who are our own flesh and blood, if their words and deeds have seriously harmed their children, and the children, to protect themselves, choose to keep their distance and step away where they should — under the moral burden and guilt of traditional filial piety, how can they settle their hearts, so as to have the wisdom to protect themselves without losing the compassion of a son or daughter?"

🔶The Bodhisattva's Teaching

The bond between parents and children is very deep.
Yet a deep bond is not always a good bond;
being of one blood does not mean never wounding each other.
People often say:

"The child comes to collect a debt, the parent comes to repay it; what was owed in a past life is repaid in this one."

This saying may serve as a metaphor; it must not be clung to as a fixed conclusion.

Since you do not know your past lives, do not use the suffering of this life to judge rashly the sins of a past one.

When parents hurt their child, do not simply say: "This is the child's debt from a past life, so it must be borne." When a child resents their parents, neither simply say: "This is the parents' past-life karma; they deserve it in this life." To speak of cause and effect like this easily uses its name to justify today's harm. Of causes unknown, make no fixed judgment; evil before your eyes should be stopped before your eyes. If parents harm their children over a long time with rage, humiliation and control, if their conduct is not good, then it is not good.

Do not, because they are parents, rename harm as loving kindness. Nor, because a child suffers, demand of them: "This is your karma; you should endure it." Patience is not letting others harm you; filial piety is not losing all boundaries; compassion is not indulging evil deeds. If drawing near only deepens anger on both sides; if every meeting stirs up harsh words again; if living together long has left body and mind without peace, then stepping away from that situation for a time is not necessarily unfilial.

To distance yourself from a harmful bond is not to cut off a kind heart. You may see them less, without cursing; you may refuse, without humiliating; you may set boundaries, without revenge; you may no longer bear their control, and still wish them peace. This is: the body may keep a distance; the heart need not hold hatred. If your parents are in need and it is within your means, and does not throw you back into harm, you may care for them in measure.

If meeting them in person would surely bring great suffering, you may find another way; you need not bear everything yourself. Filial piety must have wisdom to last. True filial piety is not children obeying whatever parents say. If parents act badly, for the child to advise them gently is also filial; if they do not listen, keep to what is right for yourself, and do not, in order to obey your parents, end up doing wrong as well. If your heart is filled with guilt: "They are my parents after all — how can I keep away?"

ask yourself: "Am I abandoning my parents, or am I stopping us from going on hurting each other?" The two are not the same. Some distance is not heartlessness; rather, it keeps both sides from creating harsh speech, anger and new grievances day after day. If stepping back a little means each creates a little less harm, this distance may also be a skillful means. Yet you should also reflect on yourself: do not, because you were hurt, make revenge your boundary; do not, because your parents erred, judge their whole life as evil.

Where they were wrong, see that it was wrong; where there was kindness, know it as kindness. That there was kindness does not mean the harm did not exist; that there was harm need not erase all the kindness that once was. When you can see both at once, the heart gradually leaves the two extremes of love and hate. As for so-called past-life cause and effect, if you believe in it, you may understand it this way: since you have met today, there are today's conditions. As for who owed whom how much in a past life, do not trouble to calculate. What truly matters is: in this life, do not keep running up debts to each other.

When parents hurt their children with harsh words, it should stop;
when children retaliate with harsh words, it too should stop.
If one side cannot change, the other can still decide
not to let harmful karma continue through themselves.
This is where karma is turned.
So I advise the wounded children:
you need not prove your pain through hatred;
nor prove your filial piety by sacrificing yourself.

What needs healing, heal; where you need help, seek help; where boundaries are needed, set boundaries; where you should step away, you may step away. But if one day your heart is strong enough and you are willing to slowly put down resentment, it is not because everything your parents did was right, but because you do not want past wounds to rule the rest of your life. If there is forgiveness, it must arise from the heart; it cannot be forced.

If you cannot yet forgive, first do not retaliate; if you cannot yet be close, first keep your distance; if you cannot yet let go, first protect yourself. Step by step — this too is practice. So you ask: how to have the wisdom to protect yourself without losing a child's compassion? I say: compassion need not mean closeness; filial piety can have boundaries. Not indulging their evil is wisdom; not wishing them to suffer is compassion; not repaying evil with evil is practice; not letting yourself be hurt again is also protecting life.

Do not use "filial piety" to force people to bear harm;
do not use "cause and effect" to find reasons for harm.
If you ask about past lives, past lives are hard to know;
if you ask about today, today the mutual wounding can stop.
Cause and effect does not tell you to endure evil;
it reminds you: do not let evil go on through yourself.

🔶Plain Rendering

The bond between parents and children is very deep. But a deep bond does not necessarily mean a good one; being related by blood does not mean you will never hurt each other.

People often say: "Children come to collect debts, parents come to repay them; what was owed in a past life is repaid in this one." This can be used as a metaphor, but it must not be held as a fixed conclusion. Since you do not know what happened in past lives, do not use this life's pain to rashly judge past-life wrongs.

When parents hurt their children, you cannot just say: "The children owed this from a past life, so they should bear it." When children resent their parents, you cannot just say: "The parents created this karma in a past life, so they deserve it now." Talking about cause and effect this way easily uses its name to justify today's harm.

Do not draw conclusions about causes you cannot know; harm happening in front of you should be stopped right there.

If parents hurt their children over a long time through angry scolding, humiliation and control, then if that behavior is not good, it is not good. You cannot rename harm as love just because they are parents. Nor can you demand of a suffering child: "This is your karma, you should endure it."

Patience is not letting others hurt you; filial piety is not losing your boundaries; compassion is not indulging harmful behavior.

If being close only makes the anger between you deeper; if every meeting brings harsh words again; if being together for a long time has already left your body and mind without peace — then leaving that environment for a while is not necessarily unfilial. Staying away from a harmful bond is not cutting off your kindness.

You can see them less, without cursing them; you can say no, without humiliating them; you can set boundaries, without seeking revenge; you can stop accepting their control and still wish them well. That is: the body can keep a distance, and the heart need not hold hatred.

If your parents need something, you can help to a reasonable degree, as long as it is within your means and does not put you back in harm's way. If direct contact will certainly cause great pain, you can find other ways; you do not have to bear everything in person. Filial piety needs wisdom in order to last.

True filial piety is not children obeying everything their parents say. If parents behave badly, gently advising them is also filial; if they do not listen, hold on to what is right for you, and do not, for the sake of obeying your parents, end up doing wrong yourself.

If guilt arises in your heart — "They are my parents after all, how can I keep away?" — ask yourself: "Am I abandoning my parents, or am I stopping us from continuing to hurt each other?" These two are not the same.

Some distance is not coldness; instead, it keeps both sides from creating harsh speech, anger and new resentments every day. If stepping back a little lets both of you do a little less harm, that distance can also be a skillful means.

But you should also look at yourself: do not make revenge your boundary because you were hurt; do not judge your parents' whole life as bad because they made mistakes. Where they were wrong, see that it was wrong; where they were kind, recognize their kindness. Having been kind does not mean the harm did not exist; having caused harm does not have to erase all the kindness there once was. When you can see both at the same time, the heart gradually leaves the two extremes of love and hate.

As for so-called past-life cause and effect, if you believe in it, you can understand it like this: since you have met today, there are today's conditions. As for who owed whom how much in a past life, do not bother calculating. What truly matters is: in this life, do not keep running up debts to each other.

When parents hurt their children with harsh words, it should stop; when children strike back with harsh words, that should stop too. If one side cannot change, the other can still decide not to let harmful karma continue through themselves. This is where karma is turned around.

So I advise children who have been hurt: you do not need hatred to prove your pain; nor do you need to sacrifice yourself to prove your filial piety. What needs healing, get it healed; where you need help, ask for help; where boundaries are needed, set them; where you need to leave, you may leave.

But if one day your heart has enough strength and you are willing to slowly put down your resentment, it is not because everything your parents did was right, but because you do not want past wounds to keep ruling the rest of your life.

If forgiveness comes, it has to come from the heart; it cannot be forced. If you cannot forgive yet, first do not retaliate; if you cannot be close yet, first keep your distance; if you cannot let go yet, first take good care of yourself. Step by step — this too is practice.

So you ask: how can I have the wisdom to protect myself without losing a child's compassion? I say:

compassion need not mean closeness; filial piety can have boundaries. Not indulging their wrongdoing is wisdom; not wishing them to suffer is compassion; not repaying harm with harm is practice; not letting yourself be hurt again is also protecting life.

Do not use "filial piety" to force people to bear harm; do not use "cause and effect" to find excuses for harm. If you ask about past lives, they are hard to know; if you ask about today, today the mutual hurting can stop.

Cause and effect does not tell you to endure harm; it reminds you not to let harm continue through yourself.
