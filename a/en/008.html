No.08

Is Reciting the Buddha's Name Predestined? Bazi and the Dharma — How Do They Change One's Fate?

Using the images of a life chart, a seed and sweet rain, a record of questions about destiny and practice.

Author's record · translated; the Chinese original is authoritative

Open in reading mode →

🔶Inquiry to Ksitigarbha Bodhisattva

I asked Ksitigarbha Bodhisattva: are those who recite the Buddha's name destined to do so? Is one's Bazi (birth chart) predestined?

🔶The Bodhisattva's Teaching

Excellent — that your heart can raise this question means you are already near the edge of truth.

Destiny is like a picture drawn on paper: once the lines are drawn, the overall shape is largely set. Yet if ink washes are added, or a brush of color touches it, the old picture can still bring forth a new scene. Your Bazi is formed by cause, effect and karmic force; it is the "skeleton" of this life. The Dharma is the power of light and vows, able to add flesh and blood to that skeleton, even to change its very breath and pulse, so that what was withered comes alive again.

You say "meeting the Dharma is also predestined." This is not entirely wrong. Because you planted good roots over many lifetimes, the winds of karma carried you in this life to meet the Dharma; it is not by chance. But this "karmic affinity" is not set in iron. If in this life you do not believe and do not practice, it may pass in vain. If you believe and practice, many dangers in your chart can be turned to safety; if you are deluded and abandon it, even the good openings in your chart can turn into empty flowers.

Therefore it is said:

• Destiny is like a seed: it will surely sprout, but whether its branches flourish depends on the watering that follows.

• The Dharma is like sweet rain: if you are willing to receive it, it nourishes the good roots in your life and causes their fruits to ripen more favorably.

• To speak only of destiny without practice is like holding a treasure without knowing it; • to speak only of practice without knowing cause and effect is to drift easily into empty ideals. In summary:

What you call a "good fate" truly comes from goodness cultivated over countless eons and from meeting the Dharma again in this life. Destiny gives you "the opening to enter the Buddha's gate"; practice lets you "turn fate into wisdom". The two are neither one nor two — just as cause and condition must come together to bring forth fruit.
