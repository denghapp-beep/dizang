No.07

[The Essence of the Diamond Sutra] "Enjoying Life Without Clinging" and "Experiencing Life Without Delusion"

Two dialogues moving from non-attachment toward staying aware within every feeling.

Author's record · translated; the Chinese original is authoritative

Open in reading mode →

🔶Inquiry to Ksitigarbha Bodhisattva

A dialogue on the Diamond Sutra.

I asked Ksitigarbha Bodhisattva: my simple understanding of the Diamond Sutra is that we must not cling to anything at all. Is this understanding right?

🔶The Bodhisattva's Teaching

You say "the Diamond Sutra teaches non-attachment." This is so, yet not complete. The meaning of the Diamond is not to cut off all dharmas, but not to be bound by dharmas. To say there should be no holding at all, and so to feel dread, is to fall into the view of emptiness as nothingness. To say one must hold to fixed dharmas in order to practice is to fall into the view of eternalism. Know then that "non-attachment" means not stagnating in existence and not departing from emptiness. The mind is like a mirror: it refuses none of the ten thousand images, and is stained by none of them. This is the meaning of "Let the mind arise without abiding anywhere."

🔶Plain Rendering

You say the Diamond Sutra teaches us not to cling. That is right, but not yet complete. The "diamond" does not mean rejecting everything; it means not being trapped by things. If you think "non-attachment" means "wanting nothing at all", or even fear the arising of any thought, you fall into a view of emptiness as nothingness — becoming passive, doing nothing, denying life.

But if you then say practice only counts when there is fixed faith, fixed practice, fixed precepts and fixed results,

you fall into the view of permanence — clinging to "one fixed method or result". True non-attachment is not getting stuck in what exists, and not running away into emptiness. Like a clear mirror, it neither refuses the images of the world nor is stained by them. This is what the Diamond Sutra means by "Let the mind arise without abiding anywhere." It means: the mind clings to nothing, yet can still freely do good, give rise to compassion and help beings.

🔶Inquiry to Ksitigarbha Bodhisattva

I replied to the Bodhisattva: I put it too simply. What I meant is to enjoy every person, event and thing in this world's life, yet not cling to any of them; to make good use of things and help others within my ability; not to cling to merit or blessings; to think only of helping, and to enjoy all the feelings this world brings us.

🔶The Bodhisattva's Teaching

Excellent, excellent. What you say is already near the state of "playful samadhi". To make use of the world without being stained, to advance and retreat in human affairs without being tied — this is what is hard and precious for a practitioner. Yet you must recognize one subtle point: it is not "enjoying without clinging", but "experiencing with awareness without being deluded". If enjoyment is the master, the mind easily chases its objects; if awareness is the master, objects enter the mind and leave no trace.

The Bodhisattva views the world like a splendid house in a dream, like the bright moon in a mirror: shine on it and it appears, cease to shine and it is gone. Knowing it is illusion, yet not weary of illusion, one cultivates the true by means of the illusory. If you can keep watchful awareness amid joy and sorrow, and not forget your original heart amid honor and disgrace, then every experience becomes practice, and every joy becomes wisdom.

🔶Plain Rendering

You put it well; this way of thinking is already close to the Bodhisattva's way of walking in the world. To enjoy all things in worldly life without being dragged along by them is truly one of the rarest skills in practice. But notice a subtle difference: it is not "enjoying without clinging", but "being aware without getting lost". If enjoyment is at the center, the mind still easily changes with circumstances; but if awareness is at the core — staying clear even while enjoying, not pulled along by the situation — then you are experiencing life with a pure mind.

The Bodhisattva sees the world like a pavilion in a dream, like the moon in a mirror — it is there, but not solid; it shines, but does not stay. The Bodhisattva knows all of this is illusion, yet still lives within it with a compassionate heart, cultivating the true within the illusory, seeing stillness within movement. If you can still watch yourself when you are happy, and still keep your original heart when things go wrong, then "enjoyment" is no longer desire, but a practice of awakening.

Author's Note

Summary of the Bodhisattva's meaning:

"Within illusion, not deluded; within joy, not attached. When awareness arises amid the dust of the world, walking in the world is walking the Way."

— If what you call "enjoying life without clinging" becomes "experiencing life without getting lost", that is the Bodhisattva's "playing in the human world": amid the flowing of all things, still abiding, unmoved, with a clear mind.
