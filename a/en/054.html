No.54

Not Speaking Up When You See Wrong — Compassion or Cowardice?

What we disagree with is not always evil, and silence is not always wrong; first tell difference from harm, advise without forcing, and let compassion never indulge evil.

Author's record · translated; the Chinese original is authoritative

Open in reading mode →

🔶Inquiry to Ksitigarbha Bodhisattva

I asked Ksitigarbha Bodhisattva:

"If a friend does something I do not agree with, but I do not stop them or advise them, is this wrong? Please, compassionate Bodhisattva, give a teaching."

🔶The Bodhisattva's Teaching

Good man,
you ask whether, when a friend's conduct
does not accord with your own mind,
and you neither stop them
nor advise them,
you fail in compassion,
or are at fault.
I now tell you:
what you disagree with is not necessarily evil;
not advising is not necessarily wrong.

Beings in the world
each have their own views,
their own habits,
their own conditions.
What you delight in,
they may not delight in;
what you believe,
they may not believe.

If whenever something does not suit you
you wish to make others change it,
this is not compassion;
it may be self-attachment.
So first ask:
is this matter truly not good,
or only different from my own view?
If it is only a difference of temperament,
of choices,
of ways of living,
harming neither oneself
nor others,
then there is no need to advise on everything.

To accept that others are different
is also practice.
Views may differ;
compassion need not lessen because of it.
Yet if what your friend does
truly harms others —
deceiving, seizing,
or doing all manner of unwholesome things —
and you know it is not right,
and have the opportunity to advise them,
then you should consider
how, with skillful means,
to help them see their fault.

At such a time, if you seek only
to preserve appearances,
knowing someone is being harmed
yet paying it no heed at all,
then reflect
whether, out of fear,
indifference, or attachment to the relationship,
you have abandoned the good you ought to do.

Yet I also tell you:
encouraging others toward good
does not mean you must argue with them;
seeing someone at fault
does not mean you must stop them by force.
Some people
wake at a single word;
some people
heed not a hundred words.

Sometimes
it is fitting to speak directly;
sometimes
one must wait for the right conditions.
So before advising someone,
observe four things:
Is there truly a fault in the matter?
Are your words true to the facts?

Is the time suitable?
Does your heart come from compassion?
If the words are true
but carry anger,
even good words can
become a weapon that wounds.
If the words are gentle
but indulge harm,
even gentleness may not
be true compassion.

Truthful without harsh speech;
compassionate without indulgence.
If you see a friend at fault,
you may first advise them privately;
there is no need to rebuke them in public
and make them lose face.
When you advise,
do not point out their faults
with a heart that thinks itself above them.

With a heart of equality,
tell them what is at stake.
You may say:
'I may not fully understand,
but I fear this may cause harm;
I hope you will think it over again.'
If they are willing to listen,
explain it kindly;
if they are not willing,
there is no need to press them in anger.

Advising is your goodwill;
changing is their choice.
Yet this too has its limits.
If someone before your eyes is being harmed,
the situation is urgent,
and you are able to help safely,
you must not use
'each has their own karma'
as a reason to stand by with folded arms.

If you can stop it safely,
stop it;
if you can seek help,
seek help;
if you cannot handle it yourself,
you can find the right people
to help stop the harm.
Respecting others' choices
is not letting others do harm.

If a friend, advised again and again, will not listen
and still does what is unwholesome,
you should also know:
compassion can always remain,
and the friendship can have boundaries.
You need not, because of years of friendship,
cover up their wrongs;
nor need you, because they erred,
hate them forever.

Do not aid their evil,
nor abandon their good;
do not indulge their conduct,
nor hate the person.
You should also look at your own heart.
Sometimes not advising
is because of wisdom,
knowing this is not the moment to speak.

Sometimes not advising
is because of fear,
afraid of losing a friend.
Sometimes not advising
is because of indifference,
thinking others' suffering
has nothing to do with you.
Outwardly it is the same silence;
inwardly it is different.

So do not only ask:
'I did not advise —
was that wrong?'
Ask further:
'Why did I not advise?
Did I know clearly there was harm?
Was I able to help?
Did my silence
let the harm continue?'
The same silence
may be wisdom,
or may be cowardice;
you must look at the heart,
and also at the result.

Good man,
practice is not
remaking all your friends
into people you approve of.
Nor is it,
on seeing what is unwholesome,
always keeping silent.
True compassion
is sometimes gentle advice;
sometimes quiet listening;
sometimes respecting differences;
sometimes firmly stopping evil.

Compassion must have wisdom;
wisdom must not be without compassion.
If you have done all your goodwill can do
and they still will not change,
you need not carry all of their consequences
upon yourself.
You can advise on the causes,
but cannot act in their place;
you can show them the road,
but cannot walk it for them.

But if beings are still being harmed,
consider what reasonable, safe help
you may still offer.
Finally, I tell you in four lines:
Seeing difference, there is no need to contend;
seeing evil, consider how to advise;
advise without forcing;
be compassionate without indulging evil.

And also:
Do not bind others with your own views;
do not cover their faults out of friendship;
advise where you can, stop where you must;
do all the good you can, and do not cling to the result.

🔶Plain Rendering

Good man, you ask: when a friend does something that does not sit right with you, if you neither stop them nor advise them, are you failing in compassion? Are you at fault? I tell you now: what you do not agree with is not necessarily evil; not giving advice is not necessarily wrong. The beings of this world each have their own views, their own habits, and their own conditions.

What you like, the other person may not like; what you believe, the other person may not believe. If you want people to change whenever something does not suit you, that is not compassion; it may be self-attachment. So first ask: is this thing actually not good in itself, or is it just different from my view? If it is only a difference of personality, a different choice, a different way of living, and it hurts neither themselves nor anyone else, then there is no need to give advice about everything.

Being able to accept that others are different from you is also a kind of practice. Views can differ; compassion need not shrink because of it. But if what your friend does truly hurts others — such as deceiving, taking what is not theirs, or doing other harmful things — and you know it is not right and have the chance to advise them, then you should think about how to use skillful means to help them see their mistake. At such a time, if you only care about keeping things pleasant, knowing someone is being harmed and ignoring it completely,

then you should reflect: is it because of fear, indifference, or clinging to the relationship that you have given up the good you should do?

But I also tell you:

encouraging someone to do good does not mean you have to argue with them; seeing someone do wrong does not mean you have to stop them by force.

Some people wake up after one sentence;
some people will not listen even after a hundred.
Sometimes it is right to speak directly;
sometimes you must wait for the right conditions.
So before advising someone, look at four things:
Is there really something wrong here?
Are your words true to the facts?
Is this the right time to say it?

Does your heart come from compassion? If the words are true but carry anger, then even well-meant words can become a weapon that hurts. If the words are gentle but let harm continue, then gentleness is not necessarily true compassion. Be truthful without harsh words; be compassionate without indulging. If you see a friend doing wrong, you can first advise them in private; there is no need to criticize them in front of others and make them lose face.

When you advise, do not point out their faults with an attitude of being above them. With a heart of equality, tell them what is at stake. You could say: "I may not fully understand, but I'm afraid this could cause harm. I hope you'll think it over again." If they are willing to listen, explain it kindly; if they are not willing, there is no need to push them with anger. Advising is your goodwill; changing is their choice. But this too has its limits.

If someone is being harmed right in front of you and the situation is urgent,

and you are able to help safely, you cannot use "everyone has their own karma" as an excuse to stand by and do nothing.

If you can safely stop it, you should stop it; if you can get help, you should get help; if you cannot handle it yourself, you can find the right people to help stop the harm. Respecting other people's choices is not letting them harm others. If your friend does not listen after repeated advice and keeps doing harmful things, you should also know: compassion can always remain, and the friendship can have boundaries. You do not need to cover up their wrongs because of years of friendship; nor do you need to hate them forever because they did wrong.

Do not help them do harm, but do not give up on the good in them; do not indulge what they do, but do not hate them as a person. You should also look at your own heart. Sometimes you do not advise out of wisdom, knowing this is not the moment to speak. Sometimes you do not advise out of fear, afraid of losing a friend. Sometimes you do not advise out of indifference, feeling that others' suffering has nothing to do with you. On the outside it is the same silence, but inside it is different. So do not only ask: "I didn't advise them — was that wrong?"

Ask further:
"Why didn't I advise them?
Did I know clearly that there was harm?
Was I able to help?
Did my silence let the harm continue?"
The same silence may be wisdom, or it may be cowardice;
you have to look at your heart, and also at the result.
Good man,
practice is not turning all your friends into people you agree with.

Nor is it keeping silent whenever you see something harmful. True compassion is sometimes gentle advice; sometimes quiet listening; sometimes respecting differences; sometimes firmly stopping harm. Compassion must have wisdom, and wisdom must not lack compassion. If you have already done everything goodwill allows and they still will not change, you do not need to carry all of their consequences on your own shoulders.

You can advise them on the causes, but you cannot act for them; you can point out the road, but you cannot walk it for them. But if beings are still being harmed, think about what reasonable, safe ways you still have to help. Finally, I tell you in four lines: Seeing difference, there is no need to contend; seeing evil, consider how to advise; advise without forcing; be compassionate without indulging evil.

And also:
Do not bind others with your own views;
do not cover their faults out of friendship;
advise where you can, stop where you must;
do all the good you can, and do not cling to the result.
