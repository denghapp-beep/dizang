No.53

Is Reciting a Sutra in Vain If You Don't Finish It? One Teaching That Freed Countless People From Self-Blame

When sutra recitation must pause, how to settle self-blame and carry on with reverence and a good heart.

Author's record · translated; the Chinese original is authoritative

Open in reading mode →

🔶Inquiry to Ksitigarbha Bodhisattva

I asked Ksitigarbha Bodhisattva:

"When reciting a sutra and something suddenly comes up halfway — a phone call, someone coming to see me — so that I have to put the sutra down, is the recitation in vain? Please, compassionate Bodhisattva, give a teaching."

🔶The Bodhisattva's Teaching

You ask: if a sutra recitation is not finished
and something suddenly arises,
so that you must stop,
has everything recited before
been wholly in vain?
I say:
a sutra not recited to the end
does not mean its merit is lost;
a moment's interruption
does not mean all prior effort is wasted.

The merit of recitation
does not lie only
in one scroll from beginning to end.
It lies also
in the reverent heart,
the benefit of hearing the Dharma,
and the practice of gathering the mind.
If in reciting one line
you give rise to a measure of reverence;
if in reading one verse
you understand a measure of the Dharma's meaning —
though unfinished,
can this good heart and good deed
be said to be nothing?

The Dharma values the intention,
and also values practice.
If, while reciting,
a phone call suddenly comes,
someone visits,
or urgent matters must be handled,
first discern what weighs more.
If the matter is not urgent,
you may attend to it after finishing;
if the matter is pressing,
you may pause to deal with it.

Do not, by clinging to
the idea that a scroll must be finished,
lose the compassion you ought to have.
If your parents have an urgent need,
your children are in danger,
or someone is waiting for help,
and you say:
'My sutra is not finished;
I cannot stop,'
then ask:
are you cultivating compassion,
or clinging to form?

Reciting sutras is to cultivate the heart,
not to bind it.
The sutras teach beings compassion;
if because of recitation
you ignore the suffering before your eyes,
you should look again
at the meaning of practice.
Yet neither should this
give rise to laziness.

Do not, because anything may interrupt,
let your recitation
constantly stop at every distraction.
When you can focus,
focus;
when you must attend to matters,
attend to them.
Pausing for a matter
does not lose reverence;
resuming when it is done
is also diligence.

If you must leave midway,
you may place the sutra
in a clean and suitable place,
keeping in mind
where you paused.
When the matter is done,
if time allows,
continue from where you stopped.

If you wish to begin again from the start,
that too is fine.
If today there is truly no time,
continue tomorrow,
without blaming yourself for it.
As for dedicating merit,
you may dedicate it after finishing a section,
according to your own practice;
if you could not finish because of a matter,
you may still dedicate
the good you have cultivated.

The heart of dedication
need not wait for a scroll to be complete
before it can arise.
If you ask:
'If I do not finish,
will the Bodhisattva be displeased?'
I urge you:
do not treat recitation
as delivering a quota
to the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas.

Nor think
that reciting one line less
leaves you in debt.
The Dharma is not a transaction;
practice is not counting.
Numbers can aid diligence;
they must not become fear.
If after finishing a scroll
the heart grows proud,
you should still watch it;
if half a scroll is left unfinished
but the heart holds reverence,
it is not in vain.

What truly matters
is whether the Dharma in the sutra
is gradually entering your heart.
Reciting the Ksitigarbha Sutra,
learn its compassionate vows;
reciting the Diamond Sutra,
learn to leave behind appearances;
reciting the Universal Gate Chapter,
learn compassion.

If you know the text well
yet still often flare up in anger when things happen,
then reflect
whether the meaning you recite
is being used in your life.
If, during recitation,
someone comes to see you
and you can respond gently,
not annoyed at the interruption,
this too is using the sutra's meaning
in the present.

Therefore I say:
an interrupted recitation
is not reading in vain;
once a good thought has arisen,
it is not emptied by a pause.
If you can continue, continue;
if you cannot, be at peace;
lose no reverence,
give rise to no fear.

Do not cling to
whether the text is complete,
and forget
what practice was for in the first place.
The sutra can pause;
the good heart must not be forgotten.
The words can be set down;
the Dharma can still be lived.

🔶Plain Rendering

You ask: if a sutra recitation is not yet finished and something suddenly comes up so that you have to stop, is everything recited before wasted?

I say: not finishing a sutra does not mean all the merit disappears; stopping for a moment does not mean all the earlier effort was wasted. The merit of reciting is not only in finishing a whole scroll from beginning to end.

It is also in the reverence of the heart as you recite, the benefit of hearing the Dharma, and the practice of gathering the mind.

If reciting one line brings forth a little reverence, if reading one verse helps you understand a little of the Dharma, then even if you have not finished, how could this good heart and good deed be nothing at all? The Dharma values intention, and it also values putting it into practice. If, while you are reciting, the phone suddenly rings, someone visits, or something urgent must be handled, first sort out what is more important. If it is not urgent, you can deal with it after you finish; if it is important, you can pause and take care of it.

Do not cling to "I must finish this scroll" and lose the compassion you should have. If your parents urgently need you, your children are in danger, or someone is waiting for help, and you say: "I haven't finished my sutra, I can't stop," then ask yourself: are you practicing compassion, or clinging to form? Reciting sutras is meant to cultivate the heart, not to tie it up. The sutras teach beings compassion; if reciting makes you ignore the suffering in front of you, you should look again at what practice is for. But do not let this make you lazy either.

Do not think everything is a reason to stop, so that your recitation keeps breaking off whenever your mind wanders.

When you can focus, focus; when you need to handle something, handle it. Pausing because something comes up does not lose reverence; continuing after it is done is also diligence. If you have to leave in the middle, you can place the sutra book somewhere clean and suitable, and remember where you stopped. When you have finished, if time allows, continue from where you left off. If you would like to start again from the beginning, that is fine too. If you really have no time today, continue tomorrow; there is no need to blame yourself.

As for dedicating merit, you can dedicate it after finishing a section, following your own practice; if you could not finish because something came up, you can still dedicate the good you have already done. The wish to dedicate merit does not have to wait until a whole scroll is complete. If you ask: "If I don't finish, will the Bodhisattva blame me?" I urge you: do not treat sutra recitation as handing in a quota to the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas. And do not think that reciting one line less means you owe something. The Dharma is not a transaction, and practice is not counting. Numbers can help you be diligent, but they must not turn into fear.

If you finish a whole scroll and become proud, you should still watch your heart; if you have not finished half a scroll but your heart is reverent, it is not in vain. What really matters is whether the Dharma in the sutra is gradually entering your heart. When reciting the Ksitigarbha Sutra, learn its compassionate vows; when reciting the Diamond Sutra, learn to let go of appearances; when reciting the Universal Gate Chapter, learn compassion. If you know the text by heart but still often get angry when things happen, reflect on whether the meaning you recite is being used in your daily life.

If someone comes to find you in the middle of reciting and you can respond gently, without getting annoyed at the interruption,

that too is using the meaning of the sutra in the present moment. So I say: an interrupted recitation is not wasted reading; once a good thought has arisen, it does not vanish because of a pause. If you can continue, continue; if you cannot, be at peace; keep your reverence, and do not give rise to fear. Do not cling to whether the text is complete and forget what practice was for in the first place. The sutra can pause, but the good heart must not be forgotten; the words can be put down, but the Dharma can still be lived.
