No.41

Ksitigarbha Bodhisattva's Teaching: Non-Self and the Causes of Goodness and Wisdom

From the relation between the "self" and good deeds, an inquiry into non-self, compassion and wisdom.

Author's record · translated; the Chinese original is authoritative

Open in reading mode →

🔶Inquiry to Ksitigarbha Bodhisattva

I asked the Bodhisattva: if there is no "self", where do goodness and wisdom come from? Is the "self" we cling to really necessary?

🔶The Bodhisattva's Teaching

"Non-self is not nothing;
self is not real.
Beings cling to self,
and so give rise to discrimination;
bodhi is free of self,
and so gives rise to compassion.
Goodness is not made by the self;
it is formed by causes and conditions.

Wisdom is not owned by the self;
the original nature is clear of itself.
If you cling to there being a self,
goodness easily becomes pride;
if you cling to there being no self,
you fall into empty stillness.
Leave the views of both extremes,
and the Middle Way appears.

What you ask now
is close to the heart of the Dharma.
Know that practice
is not destroying the body,
but breaking the attachment to self.
When attachment is gone, there is clarity;
within clarity there is compassion;
within compassion there is wisdom;
within wisdom there is no self."

🔶Plain Rendering

"'Non-self' does not mean there is nothing at all; 'self' does not mean there truly is a solid, unchanging me. Because beings cling to the idea of a 'me', they develop a discriminating mind; and bodhi-wisdom, precisely by leaving behind attachment to 'me', can give rise to true compassion.

Good deeds are not created by 'me'; they come about through the coming together of causes and conditions. Wisdom is not something 'I' own; it is the original nature naturally revealing itself.

If you cling to 'there really is a me', good deeds easily turn into pride (feeling that you are doing good and are above others); but if, the other way round, you cling to 'there is no me at all', you fall to the side of empty stillness, passivity and inaction.

Only when you leave these two extreme views does the Middle Way appear. The question you ask now is already very close to the core of the Dharma.

Know that practice is not about destroying this body (or destroying yourself as a person), but about breaking the attachment to 'me'.

Once attachment is removed, wisdom appears; within wisdom there is naturally compassion; within compassion wisdom is naturally contained; and in such wisdom there is no 'me' in charge."
